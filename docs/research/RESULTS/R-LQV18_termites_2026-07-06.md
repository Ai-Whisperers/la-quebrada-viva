## TL;DR — Yes, you can prevent them, save lightly-infested trees, and protect important ones biologically. Live with them, but manage them.

**The bad news first:** you will never eradicate termites from a 62-ha Atlantic Forest property. They are part of the ecosystem and they are doing useful work (decomposing dead wood, cycling nutrients, aerating soil). **The good news:** with the right strategy, you can:

1. **Prevent them from damaging structures** (cob cabins, bridge, dams) — through design + biological barriers
2. **Save lightly-infested trees** — through targeted treatment
3. **Protect important trees** — through a combination of biological, mechanical, and minimal-chemical methods
4. **Coexist with them in the forest** — accept their role, contain their damage to non-critical zones

## The 4 termite species you'll meet at LQV (Paraguay, Atlantic Forest, Escobar)

| Species | Type | Mound? | Damage profile | Frequency at LQV (estimated) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nasutitermes corniger** | Subterranean + arboreal | Yes (dark brown, 30-80 cm, on trees or ground) | Eats dead wood, can attack damp timber in buildings | **Very common** |
| **Coptotermes gestroi** (Asian subterranean) | Subterranean | No visible mound (carton nests in wood) | **Most destructive to buildings** — eats structural timber, hides inside wood | **Common** (introduced species, spreading in PY) |
| **Cryptotermes brevis** (drywood) | Drywood | No | Eats dry wood (furniture, beams) — small colonies inside the wood itself | **Common indoors** |
| **Microcerotermes spp.** | Subterranean + arboreal | Yes (small, ~20 cm) | Minor damage, mostly ecological role | **Very common** |

**The one to really worry about is Coptotermes gestroi** — invasive from Southeast Asia, now widespread in Paraguay's urban areas, and responsible for most of the building damage you'll read about. The native Nasutitermes is more visible (those are the mounds you'll see) but less destructive to buildings.

## Question 1 — Can we prevent termite mounds?

**Honest answer: no, you cannot prevent them from existing on a 62-ha forest property.** They are native, ecologically essential, and have been there longer than humans.

**What you CAN do:** prevent them from being a problem *near structures*.

### Prevention strategy (4 layers)

#### Layer 1 — Design choices (no chemicals, just smart construction)
- **Stainless steel termite collar** at every cabin foundation (a 15 cm wide strip of 0.5 mm stainless steel, embedded in the concrete, projecting 5 cm above ground level). Termites cannot climb past smooth metal. ~$30-50 per cabin.
- **No wood-to-soil contact anywhere.** All timber starts minimum 30 cm above grade, on stone or concrete pedestals.
- **Use termite-resistant wood** for any structural timber touching the building envelope: **lapacho negro** (Handroanthus heptaphyllus), **cedro** (Cedrela odorata), or **yvyra pyta** (Peltophorum dubium). All native to LQV's Atlantic Forest, all naturally termite-resistant due to extractives. R39 cross-ref has pricing — $10-20 per seedling, but each tree gives multiple future timber posts.
- **No buried wood.** Don't bury formwork, stakes, or scrap lumber in the ground after construction — it's a termite magnet.

#### Layer 2 — Physical barriers (mechanical, lasts 20+ years)
- **Sand barrier:** A 50 cm wide × 10 cm deep trench of graded washed sand (1-2 mm particles) around each cabin foundation. Termites cannot move sand particles they can't grip. They literally tunnel through the sand and it collapses behind them. ~$80-120 per cabin in sand + labor.
- **Basalt grit barrier:** Same as sand but with crushed basalt or granite (sharp edges). Slightly more expensive, slightly more effective. R46 can supply.
- **Stainless steel mesh** (Termi-Mesh or equivalent): 0.5 mm wire, embedded in foundation. Used in Australia for 30+ years, proven 50+ year lifespan. ~$5-8/m².

#### Layer 3 — Biological deterrents (the natural route Wes asked about)
- **Neem oil soil drench** around foundations (every 6 months). Neem (Azadirachta indica) contains azadirachtin, which disrupts termite molting and feeding. Grows well in PY. ~$10-15 per cabin per treatment.
- **Vetiver grass hedges:** Vetiveria zizanioides planted 50 cm from each cabin wall, in a continuous hedge. The roots exude nootkatone, a natural termite repellent. Plus the roots go 2-3 m deep — they create a biological barrier in the soil. ~$2-3 per linear meter of hedge.
- **Beneficial nematodes** (Steinernema carpocapsae): applied as a soil drench, they infect and kill termite larvae. Available from agricultural suppliers in Asunción. ~$30-50 per treatment per cabin, applied annually in early rainy season (October).
- **Compost top-dressing:** A healthy soil microbial community outcompetes termite-symbiotic fungi. Maintaining 5-10 cm of compost or leaf mulch around foundations actually *reduces* termite pressure long-term.

#### Layer 4 — Monitoring (so you catch them early)
- **Bait stations** (commercial: Advance, Sentricon, or DIY with cardboard + borate). Place 6-12 stations around each cabin, check monthly. When termites hit the bait, you know they're active in that zone. ~$15-25 per station, or $3-5 per DIY station.
- **Annual inspection** by a PY pest control professional (~$80-150 per visit, includes treatment if found). R39 has arborist contacts; same networks have pest controllers.

## Question 2 — Can an already-infested tree be saved?

**Short answer: YES, if caught early. NO, if the infestation is advanced.**

### Diagnostic ladder (check in this order)

1. **Look for signs:** mud tubes on the trunk (Coptotermes), small holes in the bark with sawdust-like frass (drywood), soft areas that give under hand pressure (advanced decay), sawdust at the base.
2. **Tap the trunk.** Hollow sound = internal damage, often past saving.
3. **Probe with a screwdriver.** Soft wood that penetrates easily = advanced decay. Hard wood with minor surface galleries = treatable.
4. **Look at the canopy.** Full canopy + minor trunk signs = saveable. Thinning canopy + major trunk signs = past saving.

### Treatment options (in order of intervention intensity)

#### A. Light infestation (early stage, tree otherwise healthy)
- **Direct nest removal** if the nest is visible and accessible (small mound on trunk, ~20-30 cm). Use a stick to break it open, expose the termites, **let the natural predators handle them** — ants, birds (especially woodpeckers), parasitoid flies. The colony will relocate or die.
- **Beneficial nematode injection** into the nest galleries with a syringe. ~$5-10 per treatment.
- **Boric acid paste** (low-toxicity mineral, not a synthetic pesticide) painted onto exposed galleries. Termites carry it back to the colony, disrupts their digestive system. Available at any ferretería in PY as "ácido bórico." ~$10-20 per tree.

#### B. Moderate infestation (visible damage, canopy still mostly full)
- **Trunk injection** with a systemic termiticide. The tree translocates the chemical through its sap, any termite eating the wood dies. Active ingredients: **imidacloprid** (synthetic, very effective, persistent 2-3 years) or **neem oil** (natural, less persistent, requires yearly re-treatment).
- **Professional arborist required** for trunk injection — drilling at the wrong depth kills the tree. R39 (Guyra Paraguay + Roberto Ávila + IngaVerde) has the expertise.
- Cost: $50-150 per tree, lasts 2-3 years.

#### C. Advanced infestation (major damage, thinning canopy)
- **The tree is a hazard.** Remove it. Have an arborist fell it safely.
- **Replace with a resistant species** (lapacho, cedro, yvyra pyta) from R39's nursery list.
- **Leave the wood on-site** as wildlife habitat (termites will colonize it, which actually *reduces pressure on nearby healthy trees* — it's a "sacrificial log" strategy used by permaculture designers).

### Critical rule: **don't try to save a champion tree without professional help.** Improper trunk injection kills the tree. Call Roberto Ávila or Guyra Paraguay (R39 §a) before drilling.

## Question 3 — Protecting important trees (lapachos, cedros, future specimens)

The strategy combines everything above, layered:

1. **Pre-planting site selection:** Don't plant valuable trees within 5 m of an existing structure or in a known termite zone. Use the **Guyra Paraguay species-site matching** guidance (R39 §b) — some species are less attractive to termites than others.

2. **Mulch ring (NOT touching the trunk):** 5-10 cm of compost or wood chip mulch in a 1 m radius around the tree, **but kept 15 cm away from the trunk itself.** The mulch feeds beneficial soil microbes; the gap prevents termites from using the mulch as a bridge to the trunk.

3. **Annual beneficial nematode application** (October, start of rains): $30-50 per important tree.

4. **Companion planting with repellent species:**
   - **Vetiver grass** in a 1 m radius hedge around the trunk. Same nootkatone effect as for cabins.
   - **Neem tree** planted 3-5 m upwind (azadirachtin volatilizes slightly from leaves). Don't plant too close — neem is allelopathic and will suppress the desired tree's growth.
   - **Marigolds (Tagetes)** in the mulch ring. Roots exude alpha-terthienyl, toxic to soil-dwelling termites. Annual replanting.

5. **Annual inspection** by an arborist (R39 has Roberto Ávila at Gs. 350,000/visit ≈ $50 USD). For 10 important trees: $500/year all-in.

6. **Avoid wound-sealing paint on pruning cuts.** Old wisdom was to seal cuts with pruning paint — but research in the last 20 years shows this *traps* moisture and accelerates decay. Leave cuts to heal naturally, or use a thin coat of **linseed oil + beeswax** (traditional, breathable).

## Pitfalls (real failures I've seen or read about)

1. **Boric acid on living trees with no signs of active termites.** Kills the symbiotic fungi in the soil, disrupts nutrient cycling. Only apply when you see evidence.
2. **Cement-filled trunk cavities.** Old practice, now known to trap moisture against living wood and accelerate decay. Use **wood epoxy** (e.g., Arboblend) instead if you must fill a cavity, and only after killing all the termites in it.
3. **Persistent soil-applied termiticides (chlordane, heptachlor).** Banned in most countries for good reason — they last 30+ years in soil and kill everything. If a pest-control company in PY proposes these, find another company.
4. **"Termite-proof" wood claims.** No wood is truly termite-proof. Even lapacho, the most resistant native species, will be eaten if it's the only food available. Use it + a barrier + monitoring.
5. **Removing all dead wood from the forest.** Counterproductive — you're removing their food source, so they move to your buildings. Leave coarse woody debris in the forest, keep it away from structures.
